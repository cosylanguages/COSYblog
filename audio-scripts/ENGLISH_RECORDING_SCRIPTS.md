## 📜 1. Stop Translating! 10 Reusable Sentence Frames (CEFR A0–B1)
**Audio Folder Target:** `audio/blog/stop-translating-10-sentence-frames-page-{N}.mp3`

### Page 1: Introduction to Circumlocution
**Vocal Direction:** *Warm, reassuring voice. Pause slightly before key bold terms.*
> "Welcome to Page 1 of 'Stop Translating! 10 Reusable Sentence Frames That Let You Speak From Day One'.
> One of the biggest obstacles for beginner and intermediate language learners is the habit of translating word by word. When you don't know an exact word—like corkscrew, pharmacy, or architect—your brain often freezes.
> Our methodology praises eliminating translation in favor of a creative, natural approach. Native speakers don't know every single word in the dictionary either; what they have is the ability to describe what they don't know. Circumlocution is a real communication skill!
> Master these ten reusable sentence frames and twenty survival phrases. Each frame acts like a LEGO piece: combine it with simple words to create hundreds of original sentences."

### Page 2: The Core Shift
**Vocal Direction:** *Clear tabular reading. Contrast the left column with the right column clearly.*
> "Welcome to Page 2: The Core Shift from Translation to Description.
> Here is how you shift your thinking:
> Instead of searching for basic identification... say: 'It's a...'
> Instead of searching for professions... say: 'It's a person who...'
> Instead of searching for locations... say: 'It's a place where...'
> Instead of searching for objects... say: 'It's a thing that...'
> Instead of searching for functions... say: 'It's something you use to...'
> Instead of searching for moments... say: 'It's a time when...'
> Instead of searching for dates... say: 'It's a day when...'
> To give examples, say: 'For example...'
> To explain definitions, say: 'It means...'
> And to ask for help in English, ask: 'How do you say this in English?'"

### Page 3: The 10 Essential Sentence Frames
**Vocal Direction:** *Read each numbered frame distinctly, followed by example sentences.*
> "Welcome to Page 3: The 10 Essential Sentence Frames.
> Frame 1: It's a... Examples: It's a book. It's a problem. It's a restaurant.
> Frame 2: It's a person who... Examples: It's a person who teaches children—a teacher. It's a person who helps sick people—a doctor.
> Frame 3: It's a place where... Examples: It's a place where people buy food—a market. It's a place where you sleep—a hotel room.
> Frame 4: It's a thing that... Examples: It's a thing that tells the time—a clock. It's a thing that opens doors—a key.
> Frame 5: It's something you use to... Examples: It's something you use to cut bread—a knife. It's something you use to write—a pen.
> Frame 6: It's a time when... Examples: It's a time when people sleep—nighttime.
> Frame 7: It's a day when... Examples: It's a day when families meet—a holiday.
> Frame 8: For example... Example: I like sports. For example, football and tennis.
> Frame 9: It means... Example: 'Huge' means very big.
> Frame 10: How do you say... in English?"

### Page 4: Sensory & Descriptive Anchors
**Vocal Direction:** *Dynamic, descriptive tone.*
> "Welcome to Page 4: Sensory & Descriptive Anchors.
> When an object is hard to define, anchor it with sensory descriptions:

> 'You use it to...' -> You use it to open bottles.
> 'It looks like...' -> It looks like a bird.

## 📜 3. My Top 5 Favourite English Grammar Rules (CEFR B1–B2)
**Audio Folder Target:** `audio/blog/top-5-favourite-grammar-rules-page-{N}.mp3`

### Page 1: Introduction (Grammar as a Creative Tool)
**Vocal Direction:** *Enthusiastic, welcoming host tone. Expressive and articulate.*
> "Welcome to Page 1 of 'My Top 5 Favourite English Grammar Rules for Intermediate Learners', presented by founder JY DM.
> For many language learners, grammar feels like a strict set of rules designed to catch mistakes. Traditional textbooks often focus on rigid formulas: subject plus verb plus object, strict tense charts, and endless lists of exceptions.
> However, when you reach the intermediate level, CEFR B1 to B2, grammar transforms into something much more exciting: a creative toolkit for expressing emotion, playfulness, and social connection. Native speakers constantly adapt and stretch grammar patterns to communicate subtle shades of meaning.
> Here are my top five favourite grammar rules in English that go beyond standard textbook formulas and give your intermediate English immediate natural flair."

### Page 2: Rule 1: The Softening Power of the -ish Suffix
**Vocal Direction:** *Casual, lighthearted tone emphasizing the conversational flexibility of -ish.*
> "Welcome to Page 2: Rule 1, The Softening Power of the -ish Suffix.
> In formal English, precision is valued. But in everyday casual speech, sounding too precise can sometimes feel stiff or overly formal. That is where the magical suffix '-ish' comes in.
> Adding '-ish' to numbers, times, colors, and adjectives signals approximation, flexibility, or polite hesitation.
> For approximate time and numbers: 'Let's meet at seven-ish'—meaning around 7:00, perhaps 7:05 or 7:10. 'He looks thirty-ish.'
> For colors and qualities: 'She wore a greenish sweater.' 'The movie was long-ish, but good.'
> For softening opinions and states: 'Are you hungry?' 'Hungry-ish!' Meaning a little bit hungry. 'He seemed annoyed-ish.'
> Using '-ish' allows you to sound relaxed and conversational without committing to absolute precision."

### Page 3: Rule 2: Emotional -ing with Stative Verbs
**Vocal Direction:** *Engaging, enthusiastic tone highlighting emotional immediacy.*
> "Welcome to Page 3: Rule 2, Emotional -ing with Stative Verbs.
> Standard textbook grammar teaches a clear rule: stative verbs, such as love, hate, like, understand, or want, describe permanent states and should never be used in the present continuous tense with '-ing'.
> While that rule holds true for formal academic writing, native speakers routinely break it in spoken English to convey heightened, temporary emotion.
> For present enjoyment: 'I'm loving this new podcast!' You are actively experiencing great joy right now, rather than just stating a general fact.
> For temporary dislike: 'I'm hating this rain today.' An immediate emotional reaction to the weather right now.
> For checking in: 'Are you liking the food?' Asking about your real-time experience at this moment.
> For dynamic realization: 'I'm understanding grammar much better these days!' Highlighting an ongoing process of growth.
> Shifting a stative verb into the continuous form adds warmth, vivid energy, and immediacy to your expression."

### Page 4: Rule 3: The Functional Multi-Tool: Work is Working / Work is Not Working
**Vocal Direction:** *Clear analytical tone, emphasizing semantic versatility.*
> "Welcome to Page 4: Rule 3, The Functional Multi-Tool: Work is Working versus Work is Not Working.
> The verb 'work' is one of the most powerful and versatile words in intermediate English. Beginners usually learn 'work' strictly as having a job or doing tasks at an office. But its true communicative power lies in its broader meanings: functioning, effectiveness, and suitability.
> For functioning and operating: 'The elevator isn't working today.'
> For producing desired results: 'Our study strategy is really working!'
> For convenience and suitability: 'Does Thursday at 3 PM work for you?' 'That works for me!'
> For job and productivity: 'I have a lot of work to complete.'
> This gives rise to the clever dual expression: 'Work is working'—meaning my job or project is producing great results—versus 'Work is not working'—meaning my current job routine or strategy is failing. Understanding these layers makes your English instantly more flexible."

### Page 5: Rule 4: Verb Shifting (Turning Nouns into Active Verbs)
**Vocal Direction:** *Dynamic, upbeat tone illustrating modern language creation.*
> "Welcome to Page 5: Rule 4, Verb Shifting: Turning Nouns into Active Verbs.
> English is famous for a process known as zero-derivation or 'verbing': taking a well-known noun and turning it directly into a verb without changing its form.
> This rule allows English speakers to create vivid, economical verbs on the fly to describe daily habits, modern culture, and social routines.
> For social activities: 'Let's coffee tomorrow morning.' Instead of saying 'Let's go drink coffee together'. 'We were desk-lunching all week'—eating lunch at one's desk while continuing to work.
> For modern responsibilities and technology: 'I am adulting today by organizing my taxes'—performing adult duties. 'Just Google the location.' 'She bookmarked the article for later.'
> Verb shifting makes your language feel contemporary, dynamic, and direct."

### Page 6: Rule 5: Modern Vibe Evaluators: "It's giving..."
**Vocal Direction:** *Playful, contemporary conversational tone.*
> "Welcome to Page 6: Rule 5, Modern Vibe Evaluators: 'It's giving...'.
> Language evolves rapidly, especially online and among younger generations. One of the most widespread modern grammar frames for evaluating aesthetics, moods, or attitudes is 'It's giving...'.
> This structure functions as a quick comparative shorthand meaning 'It radiates the vibe or energy of...'.
> For evaluating aesthetic and vibe: 'It's giving main character energy'—exuding confidence and spotlight presence. 'This cafe is giving 90s nostalgia'—feeling reminiscent of the 1990s.
> For evaluating quality and atmosphere: 'That outfit is giving expensive'—looking high-end and luxurious. 'The presentation is giving chaotic'—feeling messy or disorganized.
> Understanding 'it's giving...' helps you decode informal commentary across podcasts, social media, and modern conversations effortlessly."

### Page 7: Summary Practice: Combining All 5 Rules
**Vocal Direction:** *Warm, inspiring conclusion. Contrast the two example readings clearly.*
> "Welcome to Page 7: Summary Practice: Combining All 5 Rules.
> Notice how applying these five grammar rules upgrades a rigid textbook paragraph into natural, expressive B2 conversational English.
> First, the rigid textbook B1 version: 'Let us meet around seven o'clock. I am enjoying this restaurant very much. My new business plan is producing good results. We are eating lunch at our desks now. This decor looks like a luxury hotel.'
> Now, the natural expressive B2 version: 'Let's meet at seven-ish if that works for you! I'm loving this new spot. The new strategy is definitely working, and while we're desk-lunching today, notice how this place is giving luxury resort.'
> Mastering grammar isn't about avoiding mistakes: it is about choosing the right linguistic tool to make your voice memorable!"
